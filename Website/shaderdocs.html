VixForge Shader Inspector

A complete control-by-control reference for all six VixForge shaders, with every dropdown's options listed. The material inspector is driven by the VixForge Editor, and this page is laid out the same way: one section per inspector section, in the order the inspector lists them. The six share almost everything, so read the sections below for any of them and check Shader Differences for what each one adds or drops.

Built-in Render Pipeline / VRChat only - it does not compile under HDRP or URP. Every world-lighting integration (Light Volumes, LTCGI, VRSL) is fail-safe: it strips or no-ops cleanly in worlds that lack the system.

BASE

Section 1 of 13

Render mode, albedo, brightness floor, and UV animation.

Every channel dropdown offers R, G, B and A, and most also offer Auto, which reads a plain black-and-white map correctly.

  • Rendering Mode - Cutout normally reads the Albedo texture's alpha. If your texture has no alpha, turn on Use Mask Texture and give it a black and white mask instead: white keeps the pixel, black cuts it away. It sits at the very top of the inspector, on the right of the Presets row, and is drawn without a label. Options: Opaque, Cutout, Fade, Transparent
  • CutOff - Anything in your texture more see-through than this is cut away completely. Only does anything in the Cutout rendering modes.
  • Use Mask Texture - Cut the see-through part from its own texture instead of from the alpha of your main one, so you can keep the two separate.
  • Cutout Mask - Cuts holes in the material. Black is cut away. It needs a render mode that supports it, such as Cutout or Fade.
  • Mask Channel - Which channel of the mask holds the part to cut away.
  • Color - Albedo holds your colour in RGB. In Cutout its alpha is the cutout; in Fade and Transparent it multiplies the Color's opacity. Leave it empty to set opacity from the Color alpha alone.
  • Albedo - The main colour map. Everything else layers on top of this.
  • Minimum Brightness - A floor under how dark the material is allowed to get, so you never vanish completely in an unlit room. 0 lets the dark be dark.
  • Use Alpha Map - Drives opacity from a texture instead of the Albedo's alpha, handy when your Albedo has no alpha channel. Black keeps the pixel, white takes it away, so you paint over the parts you want gone. Pick the channel that holds the mask below, or turn on Invert for the usual opacity map.
  • Alpha Map - A separate map for transparency, for when the alpha in your albedo is doing something else. White is solid, black is clear.
  • Map Channel - Which channel of the alpha map holds the see-through part.
  • Invert - Off is our default: black keeps the pixel and white takes it away, so you paint over what you want gone. Turn it on for the usual opacity map that most other shaders use, where white keeps and black takes away.
  • Transparency - Fades the whole material out. 0 is solid, 1 is invisible. Only works in the Fade and Transparent modes; Opaque and Cutout ignore it.

UV Animation

  • UV Rotation - Turns your texture on the surface, in degrees.
  • UV Speed X - Slides your texture sideways, forever. 0 holds it still.
  • UV Speed Y - Slides your texture up or down, forever. 0 holds it still.

Triplanar Projection

World SurfaceWorld Fur
  • Enable - Projects your maps from three directions in world space instead of reading the mesh UVs. A wall, a floor or a rock with no usable unwrap looks right with no unwrapping work, and two pieces that meet share one continuous surface across the join.

Triplanar Settings

  • Tiling - How many times the texture repeats per metre. Higher makes the pattern smaller.
  • Edge Sharpness - How quickly one projection hands over to the next. Low gives a long soft crossfade, which suits curved and organic shapes. High snaps to whichever way the surface faces, which suits hard edges.
  • Space - World holds the pattern still while the object moves, so neighbouring pieces line up. Local carries the pattern with the object, for anything that gets picked up or animated. Options: World, Local

Tiling Breakup

World SurfaceWorld Fur
  • Enable - Hides the repeat in a tiling texture. The material shuffles the pattern across a hidden grid, so a big floor or wall stops showing you the same tile over and over. It costs framerate, so put it where the repeat actually shows.

Breakup Settings

  • Blend Contrast - Stops the shuffled copies averaging into a blur where they overlap. Turn it up if the surface looks soft, down if you can see the joins.

NORMAL MAPS

Section 2 of 13

Macro normal map and the secondary micro-detail normal.

Every Band dropdown picks what the effect follows: Bass, Low Mid, High Mid, Treble, Volume, Peak Hold. Every channel dropdown offers R, G, B and A, and most also offer Auto, which reads a plain black-and-white map correctly.

  • Normal Map - Adds fine surface detail such as creases, seams and weave without extra polygons. Use a normal map texture set to Normal map in its import settings. Normal Strength below sets how deep it looks.
  • Normal Strength - How strongly the normal map shapes the light. Above 1 exaggerates it, 0 ignores the map and lights the surface flat.
  • Card Normal Smoothing - Rounds the lighting on flat cards, so hair and fur planes catch light like the round shape they stand for rather than like the flat sheets they are. Set the middle of that shape with Smoothing Pivot below.
  • Smoothing Pivot - The point the smoothing rounds away from, in the model's own space. Put it at the centre of the head for hair, or the centre of the limb for fur.

Second Normal Map

  • Enable - A second normal map layered over the first, with its own placement. Use it to put a pattern such as scales, quilting or a weave on top of the surface you already have.

Second Normal Map Settings

  • Map - A second normal map laid over the first, for a pattern that repeats at its own scale.
  • Strength - How strongly the second map shapes the light. Above 1 exaggerates it, 0 ignores it.
  • Blend - Layered adds the second map's bumps on top of the first. Replace swaps the first map out for the second wherever the mask allows. Options: Layered, Replace
  • Flip Green - Turn this on if the bumps look pushed in instead of raised. Maps made for DirectX need it.
  • Placement - Where the map sits on the material, how big it is, and how far it is turned. Rotation is in degrees.
  • Tiling - How many times the map repeats across and down. A negative value mirrors it.
  • Movement - Drift slides the map across the material. Spin turns it, in degrees per second.
  • Mask Source - Borrows another layer's map so the second normal map only shows where that layer does. Pick an Emission layer or a Matcap, then the channel your mask sits in. That layer has to be switched on. Leave it on None to cover the whole material. Options: None, Emission 1, Emission 2, Emission 3, Emission 4, Emission 5, Matcap 1, Matcap 2, Matcap 3, Matcap 4, Matcap 5
  • Mask Channel - Which channel of that layer's map holds your mask.
  • Invert Mask - Swap which side of that borrowed mask the second map shows on.

Bent Normals

  • Enable - Bake a bent normal map next to your AO in Substance, Marmoset or Blender, then drop it here. Ambient light follows the shape of the surface instead of washing over it, so crevices stay dark and forms read solid. Ambient Shaping controls the light falling on it, Reflection Shaping controls what it reflects.

Bent Normals Settings

  • Map - A bent normal map, which leans away from nearby geometry so creases and folds catch light the way they should.
  • Ambient Shaping - How much the bent normal map steers the ambient light. It knows which way each part of the surface is open to the room, so creases take less sky and exposed areas take more.
  • Reflection Shaping - The same idea for reflections: how much the bent normal map dims what a crease is allowed to reflect. Creases reflect less of the room than a flat area does.

Micro Detail

  • Enable - A second, much finer normal map tiled over the first. It gives the surface a close-up grain that reads as real material rather than a flat sheet, and it stays sharp when the camera is right up against you.

Micro Detail Settings

  • Map - A fine normal map tiled far tighter than the main one, for pores, weave and grain seen up close.
  • Strength - How strongly the detail normal map shapes the light on top of your main one. This is the fine stuff: grain, weave, pores.
  • Band - Which part of the music the detail raises with.
  • Raise - How far the beat pushes the detail up.
  • UV Tiling - How many times the detail map repeats across the surface. Higher is finer grain.

SURFACE

Section 3 of 13

The packed PBR map, its channel routing, reflection / specular masks, and surface shaping.

Every Band dropdown picks what the effect follows: Bass, Low Mid, High Mid, Treble, Volume, Peak Hold. Every channel dropdown offers R, G, B and A, and most also offer Auto, which reads a plain black-and-white map correctly.

  • Packed PBR Mask - Poiyomi/Substance/Marmoset compatibility: one texture packs metallic, smoothness, AO and height. Match the channels under Channel Mapping. Defaults are VixenWear native (R:Met G:AO B:Disp A:Smooth).
  • Global Metallic - Scales the metallic your packed map gives, so you can dial the whole material toward or away from metal without repainting anything.
  • Global Smoothness - Scales the smoothness your packed map gives. Higher is glossier, lower is more matte.

Channel Mapping

Tell each PBR property which channel of the Packed PBR Mask to read. Leave it alone for a VixenWear-native map, or hit Mochie Setup to match a Mochie / Poiyomi map in one click.

  • Metallic Channel - Which channel of the packed map holds metallic.
  • Invert Metallic - Tick this if your map stores metal as black rather than white.
  • Smoothness Channel - Which channel of the packed map holds smoothness.
  • Channel is Roughness - Tick this if your map stores roughness rather than smoothness, which is the same information the other way up.
  • AO Channel - Which channel of the packed map holds ambient occlusion. Set it to None if your map does not carry one. Options: R, G, B, A, None
  • Mochie Metallic Map Setup - One-click: maps a Mochie/Poiyomi 'Metallic Maps' texture (R:Met G:Smooth B:Reflection A:Specular) onto the packed PBR. Drop the Mochie map into Packed PBR Mask above first.
  • Toon Standard Specular Setup - One click for a Substance ToonStandardSpecular export: red is metallic, green is smoothness, blue is occlusion. With the Packed PBR Mask slot empty, it finds that file next to your Albedo and drops it in.

Separate Maps

  • Use Metallic Map - Read metallic from its own texture instead of from the packed map. White is metal. Global Metallic still scales it, so raise that too.
  • Metallic Map - Your metallic texture. White is metal, black is not.
  • Metallic Channel - Which channel of that texture holds metallic. Usually picked for you when you drop the texture in.
  • Use Smoothness Map - Read smoothness from its own texture instead of from the packed map. Gloss and smoothness maps work as they are. For a roughness map, tick Map is Roughness below. Global Smoothness still scales it.
  • Smoothness Map - Your gloss, smoothness or roughness texture.
  • Smoothness Channel - Which channel of that texture holds smoothness. Usually picked for you when you drop the texture in.
  • Map is Roughness - Tick this for a roughness map, where white is rough. Gloss and smoothness maps leave it off.

Reflection & Specular Masks

Reads two more channels off the packed map: a Reflection Mask that dims what the surface reflects from the world (probe / environment specular, clearcoat env, Light Volume, LTCGI), and a Specular Mask that dims direct-light highlights. Matcaps keep their own masks and are unaffected. Channel defaults match Mochie packing (B reflection, A specular).

  • Enable Masks - Use one packed texture to dim reflections and highlights per area, so a worn patch can stop shining without a separate map for each.

Reflection Mask

  • Channel - Which channel of the packed mask holds the reflection amount.
  • Invert - Tick this if your mask has it the other way round, white where you want less.
  • Strength - How far the mask is allowed to dim reflections. 0 ignores it.

Specular Mask

  • Channel - Which channel of the packed mask holds the highlight amount.
  • Invert - Tick this if your mask has it the other way round, white where you want less.
  • Strength - How far the mask is allowed to dim highlights from the lights in the room. 0 ignores it.
  • Reflection From Mask - White areas of the mask become shiny metallic reflections, using the Reflection Mask channel above. The roughness is worked out from your base texture and normal map, so you do not need a smoothness map. Lower Reflection Roughness for a sharper mirror.
  • Reflection Roughness - How blurred the reflection is in the areas the mask picks out. Higher reads as scuffed, lower stays mirror-like.

Surface Shaping

Height

  • Packed Mask Channel - Which channel of the Packed PBR Mask holds height. Height drives Parallax, Displacement and Cavity. Or turn on Use Separate Map to load a standalone height texture.
  • Use Separate Map - Load a standalone height texture instead of reading it from the Packed PBR Mask. Substance and Marmoset export height as its own grayscale file, so it goes straight in.

Height Settings

  • Height Map - How high each part of the surface sits, in greyscale. White is raised. It drives parallax and displacement.
  • Map Channel - Which channel of the separate height map to read. Auto picks for you.
  • Specular Occlusion - Stops reflections showing up inside creases that could never see the room. 1 is full, 0 turns it off.
  • Cavity - Sharpens the fine detail sitting in your height source: pores, seams and stitching sink into shadow while ridges catch the light. Radius picks how fine that detail is, small for grain and skin, larger for folds. Needs a height source above.
  • Cavity Radius - How fine the detail Cavity picks out. Small catches grain and pores, large catches folds and seams.
  • Cavity Gloss - How much the cavity detail dulls the highlight in the creases as well as darkening them.
  • Cavity Reflection Blur - Uses the same cavity detail to break up the reflection. Blur roughens the reflection in the creases so it is not a clean mirror; Occlusion dims the reflection there. Both leave the raised areas sharp.
  • Cavity Reflection Occlusion - How much the cavity detail dims the reflection in the creases. Raised areas keep reflecting normally.
  • Parallax Depth - Higher gives smoother displacement but costs more framerate. It eases off automatically at a distance. Leave at 0 if you do not use Displacement.
  • Band - Which part of the music the depth moves with.
  • Thump - How far the beat pushes the depth.
  • Displacement Strength - Pushes the surface out along its normals using your height source, so folds and seams become real geometry instead of shading. It needs a height source above. Tessellation Detail sets how finely the mesh is split to carry it.
  • Tessellation Detail - Splits each triangle into smaller ones before the surface is moved, so displacement and goo land smoothly instead of blocky. Higher costs more framerate, and it only spends the detail on triangles that are large on screen. Nothing moving the surface means nothing to smooth, so this only appears once something is. Set 0 to stop Touch from splitting triangles.

SHADING

Section 4 of 13

The lighting model and every world-lighting integration, grouped together. Light Volumes, LTCGI and Stage / VRSL are each fail-safe: they strip or no-op cleanly in worlds that lack the system.

Every Band dropdown picks what the effect follows: Bass, Low Mid, High Mid, Treble, Volume, Peak Hold. Every channel dropdown offers R, G, B and A, and most also offer Auto, which reads a plain black-and-white map correctly.

Shading Options

ToonClothing ProFur ProWorld SurfaceWorld Fur

How light falls off the surface, from a hard cel edge to fully realistic shading.

  • Enable - Switch from realistic shading to a stylised look, with the light stepping between bands instead of fading.

Shading Options Settings

  • Style - Which stylised look: stepped bands, or your own ramp texture. Options: Ramp Texture, Cel Steps, Soft, Hard, Realistic

Ramp

  • Gradient - Edit the gradient inline, or drop a texture. It reads left (unlit) to right (lit). Leave it white for a clean two-tone.
  • Offset - Slides your ramp texture along, which moves where light turns into shade.

Bands

  • Count - How many bands the light steps through. 2 is the classic hard cel look.

Realistic

  • Light Wrap - Full physically based falloff, no stylising. Light Wrap softens the terminator the way skin, fabric and fur do, without losing energy.
  • Edge Softness - Style picks how light falls off. Ramp Texture reads a gradient you supply, Cel Steps posterises into flat bands, Soft stays smooth, Hard gives one crisp terminator, and Realistic leaves the physical response alone.
  • Light Wrap - How far the light carries past the point it would normally stop. Higher softens the whole look and lifts the shaded side.

Lighting

Rim light, subsurface, transmission, anisotropy and how highlights respond to the lights around you.

  • Sharper Probes - Adds contrast to the light from light probes, so the side facing the light reads brighter and the far side darker. It is a look rather than a closer match: one strong light comes out up to a third brighter than Unity draws it. With Unified Light on it keeps that contrast and follows Soften and Hardness.
  • Unified Light - Experimental, so you can compare it with the lighting you have now. It lights you the same way whichever route a light takes to reach you: realtime, the cheaper per-vertex lights, light probes or Light Volumes. Soften and Hardness then reach every light, Light Volumes look less flat, and the per-vertex lights pick up your normal map and highlights.

Rim Light

  • Strength - How bright that edge gets. 0 turns it off.
  • Band - Which part of the music the edge listens to.
  • Flare - How far the beat pushes the edge brightness up.
  • Width - How far in from your outline the edge reaches, in tenths of the way to the middle. 1 is a thin line, 10 covers the whole surface.
  • Blur - How soft the inner side of the edge is. 0 is a crisp band, 1 fades all the way from your outline to Width.
  • Follow Light - Ties the edge to where the light comes from. At 0 the whole outline glows evenly. At 1 the lights behind and beside you light the edge the way a real rim light does, a light in front of you barely touches it, and detail in your height map shadows it.
  • Wrap - How big the light looks to the edge. Low is a small, sharp light that reaches only the side facing it, high a large, soft one that wraps further round you.
  • Scene Lights - How much the lights in the room feed the edge. 1 is full, 0 leaves them out.
  • Ambient - How much the ambient light and light probes feed the edge. 1 is full, 0 leaves them out.
  • Light Volumes - How much of the room's Light Volume feeds the edge. A volume stands in for the probe light rather than adding beside it, so this trims its share of Ambient above. 1 is full, 0 leaves them out.
  • LTCGI - How much a lit LTCGI screen feeds the edge. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • VRSL - How much the stage rig feeds the edge. It takes its light from GI Wash Overrides in the VRSL section, so raise Intensity there first.

Subsurface

  • Strength - How much light soaks in. 0 turns it off.
  • Distance - How far the light spreads once it is inside. Small keeps the glow tight to the lit edge, large carries it round onto the shaded side.
  • Power - How much extra you get looking towards the light, the way a hand held up to a lamp glows at the edges. Low shows it across a wide range of angles, high only when the light is nearly behind.
  • Thickness - How thick the material is where light soaks through. Thin parts pass more light and glow at the edges. Leave it at 0 to keep the fully thick look every material has had until now.
  • Thickness Map - Paints the thickness across the mesh instead of one value for the whole material. White is thick, black is thin. It scales the slider above, so raise that first.
  • Thickness Channel - Which channel of the map holds the thickness. Auto reads a plain greyscale map.

Transmission

  • Strength - How much light comes through. 0 turns it off.
  • Band - Which part of the music the glow listens to.
  • Glow Through - How far the beat pushes the glow up.
  • Distance - How deep the light gets before the colour of the material soaks it up. Small gives a strong tint, large lets more of the light through as it was.
  • Falloff - How much you have to be facing the light to see the glow. Low shows it from most angles, high only when the light is almost directly behind.
  • Fracture - Hold a hand up to the sun and the light does not come through evenly, it comes through as rays: bright where it found a thin path, dark where a knuckle blocked it. This makes the glow follow the detail in your height source that way, and the shafts stretch along the light rather than spreading evenly. Needs a height source. Starts at 0, which is the flat glow you have now.
  • Spread - How soft the edge of each shaft is. Low gives hard edged rays as if from a small bright source, high spreads them out the way a wide or overcast one does.

Anisotropy

  • Strength - How far the highlight stretches. 0 leaves it round. Negative stretches it the other way across the surface.
  • Rotation - Which way the brushing runs, in degrees.

Specular Response

  • Multi-Scatter Compensation - Puts back the light that a rough surface bounces around inside itself before it escapes. Without it rough metal comes out darker than it should. Leave it on unless you want the older, darker look.
  • Horizon Occlusion - Stops reflections leaking through the surface when you look at it edge-on. 1 is full, 0 is off.
  • Highlight Clamp - Tames the hotspot on very glossy material so it stops sparkling. 1 leaves it alone, lower softens the highlight, and the bottom of the slider takes it out. Scene lights only, so reflections are not affected.

Reflection Clamp

  • Amount - Eases down hotspots in light bouncing off the world: reflection probes, Light Volumes, LTCGI and stage lighting. 1 leaves them alone, lower softens them. The sliders below let a source out of it.
  • Reflection Probes - How much of the clamp reaches reflection probes. 1 eases them down with everything else, 0 leaves them alone.
  • Light Volumes - How much of the clamp reaches Light Volumes. 1 eases them down with everything else, 0 leaves them alone.
  • LTCGI - How much of the clamp reaches LTCGI. 1 eases them down with everything else, 0 leaves them alone.
  • VRSL - How much of the clamp reaches stage lighting. 1 eases them down with everything else, 0 leaves them alone.

Micro Lighting

  • Peak Light - Brightens the raised detail so peaks catch a little more light. It is the light-side companion to Micro Shading in Shadow Detail, which darkens the creases. The sliders below trim it per lighting source.
  • Scene Lights - How much of it lands on light from the lights in the room. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • Ambient - How much of it lands on ambient light and light probes. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • Light Volumes - How much of it lands on Light Volumes. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • LTCGI - How much of it lands on LTCGI. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • VRSL - How much of it lands on stage lighting. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.

Cavity Lighting

  • Strength - Raised areas catch the light and creases fall into shadow as the light rakes across, on top of the flat Cavity shading. Needs Cavity turned on in the Surface section. The sliders below trim it per lighting source.
  • Scene Lights - How much of it lands on light from the lights in the room. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • Ambient - How much of it lands on ambient light and light probes. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • Light Volumes - How much the creases react to the room's Light Volume. They did not react to it at all before, so in a room lit only by volumes the cavity detail sat flat however the light moved. Starts at 0.
  • LTCGI - How much the creases react to LTCGI. Starts at 0.
  • VRSL - How much the creases react to the stage rig. Starts at 0.

World Post Processing

World Post Processing Settings

  • Brightness Limit - Keeps highlights from going brighter than this, so the world's bloom can't turn a small glint into a huge glow. It starts easing off at half this value, so nothing clips flat.

Shadow Detail

Occlusion and shadow, gathered in one place. AO Strength and Shadow Hardness always apply; the dedicated maps below stay off until you enable them.

  • AO Strength - How strongly the ambient occlusion in your packed map darkens the creases.

Micro Shading

  • Strength - Makes occlusion react to where the light is coming from, so creases go dark when the light rakes in from the side and open up when it hits head on. Without it occlusion dims everything evenly and reads as dirt painted on. The sliders below trim it per lighting system, so you can lean on it in the ambient and ease off under a stage rig.
  • Scene Lights - How much Micro Shading lands on light from the lights in the room. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • Ambient - How much of it lands on ambient light and light probes. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • Light Volumes - How much of it lands on Light Volumes. 1 is full, 0 leaves them out.
  • LTCGI - How much of it lands on LTCGI. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • VRSL - How much of it lands on stage lighting. 1 is full, 0 leaves it out.
  • Assumed Direction - Scene lights, ambient and Light Volumes tell the material which way their light arrives. LTCGI and VRSL do not, so pick what to assume for those two. Above suits ceiling panels and overhead rigs. Camera keys it to wherever you are looking from. Surface keeps it flat and just deepens the creases. Options: Above, Camera, Surface
  • Enable Occlusion Map - Read occlusion from its own texture instead of from a channel of your packed map.
  • Occlusion Map - A baked map of where ambient light cannot reach. Black is buried, white is open.
  • Occlusion Channel - Which channel of that texture holds the occlusion.
  • Occlusion Strength - How strongly that map darkens the creases.
  • Shadow Hardness - Sharpens the edge between lit and unlit. A light already gives you the sharpest edge the shape of your mesh allows, so turning this up brightens the surface just inside that edge rather than moving the edge itself. That is a stylised look rather than a realistic one. It eases back to normal as a surface turns to face the light. 0 is off.
  • Soften Terminator - Widens the edge between lit and unlit, the way a large soft light source does. The lit side gives a little and the shaded side gains a little, so it reads as a wider gradient instead of the whole object going dim. Useful on a low poly mesh where that edge runs along the facets. 1 wraps the light all the way around. 0 is off.
  • Shadow Normal Bias - Tucks this mesh slightly inward as it casts its shadow, so a surface stops shadowing itself in speckles or stripes. Use it when one mesh needs more than the light gives everything else. 0 leaves it to the light.
  • Normal Bias Mode - Relative adds to the Normal Bias set on the light, so it keeps up with your shadow quality, and at 1 it adds three times what the light uses. Fixed moves a set distance instead, up to 2 cm at 1. Both need the light to have some bias of its own. Options: Relative, Fixed
  • Enable Baked Shadow Map - Paint shadows on permanently from a texture, for detail no light in the room would ever give you.
  • Baked Shadow Map - A baked shadow painted into the surface. Black is in shadow.
  • Shadow Channel - Which channel of that texture holds the painted shadow.
  • Shadow Strength - How dark those painted shadows go.
  • Band - Which part of the music the painted shadows deepen with.
  • Deepen - How far the beat deepens them.
  • Shadow Affects Ambient - Whether the painted shadows also darken the ambient light, not just the lights in the room. Higher reads heavier and more enclosed.

Baked Lighting

World SurfaceWorld Fur

How the material behaves when you bake a scene's lighting. Set Global Illumination to Baked on the renderer and this material's glow lights the room when you bake; on Realtime or None it stays out of the bake.

  • Emitted Light - How bright the glow is as a light source in the bake. It does not change how the material looks on screen, so turn it up when a neon sign reads right but barely lights anything, and down when it floods the room.
  • Smooth Filter - Reads the baked lightmap with a smoother filter so a low resolution bake stops looking blocky. Costs a few extra reads and no extra memory.
  • Highlight - Lets baked light throw a specular highlight, so a polished floor is not flat under a bake. Set Directional Mode to Directional in your Lighting settings or this does nothing.
  • Highlight Softness - Caps how sharp the baked highlight can get. Lower is softer and hides the resolution of the lightmap.
  • Realtime Global Illumination - Matches the Realtime Global Illumination tick in your Lighting window, and keeps itself in step with it, so you should not need to touch it. On, the material reads the realtime lightmap. Off, locking strips that out and the material locks faster.

Light Volumes V3

Light from the world's VRC Light Volumes, including its point, spot and area lights.

  • Enable - Take light from the world's Light Volumes, which is how a lot of worlds light you properly instead of falling back to one flat probe reading.

Light Volumes V3 Settings

  • Intensity - How much of the world's Light Volume lighting reaches the material. Turn it down if a world overpowers your look, or to 0 to ignore volumes entirely.
  • Specular Mix - How much the volume is allowed to put a highlight on you, on top of lighting you.
  • Fresnel - Makes the Light Volume highlight stronger at glancing angles, the way real gloss and latex catch coloured light along their edges. It matters most on dark, glossy materials, where the highlight is nearly all the colour you see. 0 keeps the flat highlight.
  • Clearcoat Specular - How much the volume shows up in your clear coat as well as in the surface under it.
  • Clearcoat Tightness - How tight that coat highlight is. Higher is a smaller, sharper glint.
  • Dominant Mode Specular - Work the highlight out from the single strongest direction in the volume rather than the whole spread. Crisper, and usually what you want on a glossy material.
  • Per-Light Specular - Gives every point light in the volume its own sharp highlight, shaped by how big the light is. Off, point lights still add to the softer highlight the volume gives you.
  • Point Light Strength - How strongly the point lights placed in the world reach this material. They used to arrive welded to the baked volumes, so the only way to bring one down was the Light Volumes strength above, and that pulls the whole room down with it. This moves the point lights on their own. 1 leaves them exactly as the world author placed them, above 1 leans into them, 0 takes them out.

  • Flow Speed - How fast they run.
  • Strength - How pronounced the drips are.
  • Bump - How much each drip stands out from the surface.
  • Gravity Flow - At 0 the streaks run down the texture. Turn it up and they run down in the world instead, so they stay vertical however the UVs are laid out and however you tilt.

Droplets

  • Amount - How much the drips stand off the surface as real shapes.
  • Scale - How big those standing drops are.
  • Glassiness - How glassy they look.
  • Fall Length - How fast they fall once they let go.
  • Sway / Wobble - Sway adds surface-tension wobble to falling drops. Surface Slide makes an attached drop run down ALONG the body before detaching. Floor Splat pins drops to the shared world floor set under Goo Effects > Floor, and spreads them into a fading puddle.
  • Surface Slide - How closely they follow your shape rather than falling straight.
  • Floor Splat - Let the drips splash when they reach the floor.
  • From Writhe - Makes drops fall from wherever the writhing has drawn the material out, instead of at an even rate all over. The reaching places shed first and most, so a drop looks like it came off the tip of a tendril rather than appearing beside it. Needs Writhe under Goo Effects to be doing something. Leave at 0 and the droplets behave exactly as they always have.
  • Pooling - How much the drops build up where they land. At 0 each one spreads and fades on the spot, so the floor stays clear. Turn it up and the splats stay wider for longer and overlap, so the ground reads as goo gathering under you rather than drops blinking out one at a time.
  • Room Lighting - Lights the drops with the room, the same way as the rest of you, so they sit in a dark club instead of glowing. At 0 they look the way they did before this option existed. Latex Ultra
  • Beat Shake-off - A big peak in the music shakes a shower of extra drops loose, water and goo, and throws the falling ones outward. Amount is how many, Threshold is how big the peak has to be, Hold is how long the shower lasts, and Softness fades it in and out. Latex Ultra
  • Custom Particle - Use your own image for each drop instead of the built-in bead, so the same physics can throw sweat, bubbles, sparks, petals or anything else off you. The alpha decides the shape, so give it a transparent background. Everything above still applies: they swell, pinch off, fall and dry out exactly as before.

Droplets Settings

  • Particle - Colour (RGB) Shape (A). Drawn upright with the drop's neck at the top, so paint it the way a drop hangs.
  • Blend - Mix between the built-in bead and your image. Part way keeps the bead's shine over your shape.
  • Spin - Turns each one a random amount so they do not all face the same way. Leave at 0 for anything that should stay upright.

Goo Effects

Latex UltraWorld Surface

  • Enable - Makes the latex sag and run downwards like it is melting. Melt To Ground, under Floor, pulls it toward the ground: set Height there to your world's floor level, usually 0. Turn Tessellation up for smoother results.
  • Mask - Where the goo sits. White is covered, black stays bare.
  • Mask Channel - Which channel of the mask says where the goo runs.
  • Extra Mask - Borrows an Emission layer Map or a Matcap mask as a second mask, so the goo only runs where both allow it, drops and strands included. It costs no extra texture. That layer has to be switched on. Options: None, Emission 1, Emission 2, Emission 3, Emission 4, Emission 5, Matcap 1, Matcap 2, Matcap 3, Matcap 4, Matcap 5
  • Extra Mask Channel - Which channel of the extra mask says where the goo runs.

Noise

  • Beat Stretch - The beat draws the strands out longer.
  • Strand Variation - How much one strand differs from the next, so they do not all move together.
  • Beat Variation - The beat spreads the strands apart, so more of them break rank and run.

  • Beat Flow - The beat hurries the flow along. It adds to the clock rather than scaling it, so the goo gathers pace and settles again without jumping.

  • Beat Underside - The beat pushes the goo toward the undersides, so it gathers where it would hang.

Sway

  • Speed - How fast it swings.
  • Surface Follow - How closely the goo hugs your shape rather than hanging straight down.

Writhe

  • Amount - How far the surface pushes out and pulls in. Small is a slow churn under the material; large is full lumps rising and travelling.
  • Beat Depth - The beat swells the writhe itself, so the tendrils surge and stretch on the hit instead of only running faster.
  • Scale - How big each swelling is. Low gives a few large masses moving through you; high gives a fine boil all over. The push shrinks as you turn this up so the surface keeps its shape, so if a fine setting looks too tame, raise Amount rather than fighting it here.
  • Speed - How fast the goo churns inside itself.
  • Beat Writhe - The goo writhes harder while the music plays and settles back to its own pace when it stops. Needs Writhe turned up.
  • Tendrils - At 0 the whole surface rolls, as much sinking as rising. Turn it up and most of it goes still while a few places reach out a long way, which is what makes them read as tendrils rather than lumps.
  • Beat Tendrils - The beat pushes the writhe from a rolling boil toward sharp tendrils.
  • Crawl - Lets the swellings travel across your body instead of pulsing where they sit. This is most of what makes it look alive.
  • Beat Crawl - The beat slides the heaped goo across the surface harder.
  • Surge - Gathers the movement into a band that climbs up you, so it arrives in waves rather than simmering evenly everywhere.
  • Fold - Stops the movement travelling one way. At 0 the swellings all drift in the same direction at the same speed, and after a few passes that reads as a loop. Turn it up and the material folds over itself instead: swellings turn, stall, double back and pull apart, and it never repeats. This is the one to reach for first if the writhing looks like it is on a timer.
  • Beat Fold - The beat folds the writhe in on itself, so it churns rather than drifting.
  • Wander - Lets the swellings push sideways, up and underneath instead of only straight out from your body. It is the difference between a surface breathing and goo reaching somewhere, and because the direction keeps turning, a swelling will change course halfway and zig-zag.
  • Beat Wander - The beat leans the writhe further into the flow, so swellings reach sideways and under instead of straight out.
  • Stretch - Pulls the swellings long instead of round. They lengthen whichever way they are travelling and narrow across it, so the material reads as being drawn out rather than as lumps inflating. Pairs with Tendrils: Tendrils picks which places reach, this decides how far they draw out when they do.
  • Run - Lets the writhing feed the melt, so wherever the material has been drawn out thin it also sags and runs. That is what turns a tendril that waves into one that hangs, runs and drips off its own tip. Needs Sag Distance under Melt to be above 0, since this decides how much the stretching adds to the sag rather than creating it.
  • Beat Run - The beat lets the stretched goo give way and run further down.
  • Wetness - Makes the swellings themselves read as thick wet goo: dark, glossy and deep where the material heaps up, thinner between, with the veins brightening along anything drawn out. This is on by default, so turning Amount up is all you need for the look. It borrows its colour and gloss from Slime Shading below, so setting those changes both together.
  • Beat Wetness - The beat wets the stretched strands, so the writhe reads slicker as the music hits.

Oobleck Dance

Floor

  • Beat Melt - The beat pulls the goo further down toward the floor.
  • Source - Where the floor is. Avatar Feet finds it from the avatar itself, so the goo lands on the ground in any world and keeps up when you walk up stairs. Manual Height lets you type a world height instead, for a prop that is not worn. Options: Avatar Feet, Manual Height

  • Contact - How many strands make it all the way down. At 1 every strand reaches the floor and pools. Lower it and only the longest get there, leaving the rest hanging as drips.
  • Beat Contact - The beat sends more strands the whole way to the floor.
  • Collision - Let the goo pile up when it reaches the floor instead of passing through it.
  • Pooling - How much it spreads out into a pool where it lands.
  • Beat Pooling - The beat spreads the pool wider where the goo lands.
  • Pool Softness - How far the goo eases into the floor instead of snapping flat onto it. Higher rounds off the join where a strand meets the pool, which is what stops it breaking into hard edged patches. At 0 it lands hard, the way it did before.

Formation

  • Beat Reach - The beat raises how far the formation front can climb.
  • Edge - How deep the wet band is at the leading edge. Small keeps a sharp waterline; large leaves a long wet gradient behind the front.
  • Beat Edge - The beat widens the band at the formation front, so the boundary softens on the hit.
  • Break-up - How wild the leading edge gets. The goo climbs in tendrils that reach, sag and swing instead of rising as a flat line. Turn it up and a few strands go a long way ahead of the rest.
  • Beat Break-up - The beat tears the formation edge into a rougher waterline.

  • Beat Drip - The beat stretches the formation cells into longer hanging strands.
  • Grab - How far the goo reaches up past the edge as it climbs, so it looks like it is grabbing the next part of you rather than just appearing.
  • Beat Grab - The beat makes the front reach further ahead of itself for the next piece of the body.
  • Lift - How far the strands pull away from your body and settle back as they climb. This is what makes them separate tendrils reaching out and taking hold, instead of a pattern sliding across the surface. Keep it small on tight gear so the goo does not lift through clothing above it.
  • Beat Lift - The beat lifts the strands at the front higher off the surface.

Touch Settings

  • Native Touch - Finds hands, and anything else close to you, from what the camera already sees, so no contacts or animator are needed. Each person sees only their own hands, only up close, and never in a mirror. The world has to draw a depth texture, which most worlds with realtime shadows do.
  • Sensing - Strength is how hard a hand presses. Reach is how far in front of you, in centimetres, a hand still counts. Radius is how wide an area each point feels around it. Max Distance is how close the viewer has to be, in metres, before it switches on, which keeps the cost down.
  • Zones - Four touchable areas, one per colour channel: red, green, blue and alpha. Paint soft edges rather than hard ones, since the material pushes in across the whole shape you paint.
  • Press Red - Animate this from a contact receiver. 0 is untouched, 1 is pressed all the way in. Give it a curve that overshoots and settles back rather than a straight ramp and the material springs like real slime.
  • Press Green - How hard a press at this contact point pushes into the goo. Driven by the contacts on your avatar, so it reacts to being touched.
  • Press Blue - How hard a press at this contact point pushes into the goo. Driven by the contacts on your avatar, so it reacts to being touched.
  • Press Alpha - How hard a press at this contact point pushes into the goo. Driven by the contacts on your avatar, so it reacts to being touched.
  • Depth - How far the material pushes in, in metres. Turn Tessellation up for smoother results.
  • Spread - How far the dent softens out past the painted zone. Low is a sharp fingertip press, high is a broad squash as though something heavy leaned on you.
  • Rim - How much material piles up around the edge of the press. This is what makes it read as something soft being squeezed rather than a dent stamped into a solid.
  • Shading - How strongly the dent shows up in the lighting. Leave this up: without it the surface moves but still catches light as though it were flat.
  • Tendrils - Strands of goo squeezed out around the contact. They grow at the rim rather than under the press, the way slime escapes around a finger.
  • Wetness - How much glossier the material goes where it is pressed.
  • Flow - Slides the slime pattern out of the way of the press, so the surface looks pushed rather than printed on. Needs Slime Shading above to be doing something.
  • Veins - Brightens the glowing veins where the material is squeezed. Needs Veins above to be turned up.

Glitter

Tiny flakes scattered across the material, each tilted its own way, so they catch the light one at a time and twinkle as you or the light moves. They are generated in the shader, so there is no texture to make and no resolution to run out of. Once flakes get too small on screen to draw individually they settle into a smooth sheen, so glitter never turns into crawling noise at a distance.

Glitter Settings

  • Anchor - Whether the flakes are fixed to the surface or to the world, so they can either travel with you or shimmer as you move past them. Options: UV, World Space
  • Sharpness - How briefly each flake flashes as you move. High is a hard twinkle, low a softer shimmer. It changes the character, not how much glitter you get.
  • Lit By - Which lights the flakes are allowed to catch. Options: Natural, Light Volumes, LTCGI, VRSL, Everything, Always Lit
  • Brightness - How brightly the flakes flash. Each layer's Strength scales it again.
  • Ambient Sparkle - How much the flakes catch the room around you rather than only direct light.
  • Minimum Light - A floor under how dim the flakes go, so they still read in a dark room.
  • Take Light Colour - How much each flake takes the colour of the light hitting it rather than its own.
  • Over Clearcoat - Put the flakes on top of the clear coat rather than under it, so they look sprinkled on rather than sealed in.
  • Mask - Where the flakes go. White carries glitter, black carries none.
  • Mask Channel - Which channel of the mask says where the glitter goes.

Layer 1

  • Density - How many flakes there are. Higher packs them in finer.
  • Flake Size - How big each flake is. Above 1 a flake grows past its own spot and overlaps its neighbours, which costs more to draw.
  • Shape - The outline of every flake. Round is the classic sparkle. Star and Cross throw a sharp glint, Shard reads as broken foil and Ring as tiny bubbles. Texture reads your own sprite from the Shape Sprite slot below, so you can draw your own. Options: Round, Hexagon, Diamond, Star, Cross, Shard, Ring, Texture
  • Shape Sprite - Your own flake shape, drawn as a white blob on black. Pick the channel it lives in below.
  • Shape Channel - Which channel of the Shape Sprite holds your flake. Most sprites use Red.
  • Coverage - How much of the surface carries glitter at all.
  • Scatter - How far each flake tilts away from the surface. Low keeps them facing out together, so they flash as one sheet. High tilts them every which way, so they catch the light one at a time.
  • Depth - Sinks the flakes below the surface, so they slide as you move your head the way real flake does under a clear coat. Higher sits deeper.
  • Colour - Where each flake gets its colour. Tint uses your colour. Iridescent gives every flake its own rainbow hue. ColorChord takes the colours AudioLink picks out of the music. Prism sweeps each flake through the spectrum as it turns. Two Tone gives every flake either your Tint or a Second Colour. Notes gives every flake one of the notes AudioLink hears, so the glitter shows the chord being played. Options: Tint, Iridescent, ColorChord, Prism, Two Tone, Notes
  • Tint - Tints the flakes.
  • Rainbow Amount - How much each flake carries its own colour, for a holographic finish rather than a plain metallic one.
  • Second Colour - The other colour a Two Tone flake can take.
  • Second Colour Share - How many of the flakes take the Second Colour. 0.5 splits them evenly.
  • Scroll - Drifts the field on forever. 0 holds it still.
  • Spin - Keeps the whole field of flakes turning. 0 holds it still.
  • Angle - Turns the whole field of flakes to a set angle, in degrees. It also points the Starburst rays.
  • Swirl - Drags the flakes into flowing veins instead of an even scatter, for a marbled, liquid look. Turn it up and the field pulls apart into rivers.
  • Swirl Scale - How big the swirls are. Low gives a few broad rivers, high gives fine curling detail.
  • Twinkle - Every flake blinks on its own clock, so the material shimmers even when you and the light are both still.
  • Twinkle Rate - How fast the flakes blink.

Style

  • Strength - How bright this layer's flakes are, on top of Brightness above. Let one layer blaze while another only glints.

Viewer Context

The material can tell where it is being drawn and who is looking at it, and react to that on its own. Nothing here needs an animator, a menu or a parameter, and everyone sees their own version at the same moment.

  • Show - Which situation changes the material: being looked at, being close, being hidden. Options: Anywhere, Only in Mirrors, Never in Mirrors, Only in Photos, Never in Photos, Only in VR, Only on Desktop
  • Seen By - Reacts to someone actually looking at you. Options: Both Eyes, Left Eye Only, Right Eye Only
  • Distance - Whether it reacts to them coming closer or moving away. Options: Off, Closer is Stronger, Further is Stronger
  • Near Distance - The distance it has fully reacted by.
  • Far Distance - The distance it has stopped reacting by.
  • Face-On Only - How square on they have to be looking before it counts.
  • Hidden Level - Reacts to being out of sight rather than in it.
  • Affects - What changes when that happens. Options: Emission, Decals, Emission and Decals
  • Also Hide Outlines - Applies the same rules above to the outline passes, so a line stops showing up alone once what it traces is hidden.
  • Also Apply To Wet And Goo - Applies the same rules above to the wet look and the goo, their drops and strands included, so they can show only in a mirror, only in photos, or only up close. Latex UltraWorld Surface
  • Reaction Glow - How far it changes.
  • Mask - Keeps the whole effect to part of the material. White is where it applies, black is left completely alone, so one patch can vanish in mirrors while the rest stays normal. The Reaction Glow follows the mask too.
  • Mask Channel - Which channel of the mask says which parts react.

  • Band - Which part of the music this reacts to.
  • Rush - How far the beat pushes this.
  • Tint - Tints the frames. White leaves them alone.
  • Position - Where the sequence sits on the surface.
  • Rotation - Turns it, in degrees.
  • Rotation Speed - Spins it on forever. 0 holds it still.

Infinity Mirror

Infinity Mirror Settings

  • Pattern - What is reflected: a ring of lights, a grid of dots, or your own texture. Options: Ring of Lights, Dot Grid, Texture

  • Pattern Texture - The shape repeated down the tunnel. It is read only when Pattern is set to Texture.
  • Glow Colour - The colour of the lights inside the mirror.
  • Reflections - How many reflections deep the mirror goes. More is deeper and costs more to draw.
  • Depth - How far apart those reflections sit, which sets how long the tunnel reads.
  • Fade Per Layer - How quickly they fade out as they recede.
  • Recession - How quickly each reflection shrinks into the distance.

  • Motion - Sets the whole stack moving. Drift slides it, and every layer deeper slides further, so it falls away from you. Spin turns it into a vortex. Flow falls the whole tunnel inward and never ends, so it keeps dropping into itself. Spin is in degrees per second, Flow in layers per second.
  • Opens Up Close - Makes the portal flat from across the room and deep once someone walks up to it.
  • Full Depth Distance - How close someone has to be, in metres, before the portal is at full depth.
  • Approach Curve - Low opens it gradually across the room. High keeps it flat until they are almost touching it.
  • Rainbow Depth - Shifts each reflection's colour a little further round the wheel.

  • Mask Channel - Which channel of the mask says where the mirror shows.

The master AudioLink toggle and every reactive sub-group beneath it.

Most of the effects below also carry a Mask Source and Mask Channel, which borrow an Emission or Matcap layer's map so the effect only shows where that layer does. The layer you point at has to be switched on. Leave it on None and the effect covers everything. Mask Source options: None, Emission 1, Emission 2, Emission 3, Emission 4, Emission 5, Matcap 1, Matcap 2, Matcap 3, Matcap 4, Matcap 5 Mask Channel options: R, G, B, A, Auto

Every Band dropdown picks what the effect follows: Bass, Low Mid, High Mid, Treble, Volume, Peak Hold. Every channel dropdown offers R, G, B and A, and most also offer Auto, which reads a plain black-and-white map correctly.

Response

If the music keeps blowing your effects out, this is the fix. Switch Mode to Calibrated and set the two cutoffs to match how loud the room actually is.

  • Enable - Let the material react to whatever music is playing, through AudioLink. The world needs AudioLink in it, and something has to be playing.
  • Mode - Classic reacts the way every VixForge material always has, so your existing looks stay put. Calibrated hands you the controls below. Options: Classic, Calibrated
  • Quiet Cutoff - Anything quieter than this counts as silence. Raise it until the material stops glowing between beats.
  • Loud Cutoff - The level that counts as full. Raise it if every beat pins the effect at maximum. Lower it if the effect never gets there.
  • Gain - Multiplies the level before the cutoffs are applied. Handy for a quiet track.
  • Shape - Left is a soft swell. Middle is a straight response. Right is punchy, so only the peaks come through.
  • Steady Autocorrelator - The autocorrelator readouts grow with how loud the track is, so a quiet passage barely shows and a loud one pins them. Turn this on to hold them at one strength whatever the volume. Off is the look every material has had.

  • Quiet Room - How many people counts as empty.
  • Packed Room - How many people counts as full.

SHADER DIFFERENCES

Six Shaders

What each of the six adds and drops. Everything not listed here is shared, so every section above applies to all of them.

All six run on one core, so the PBR base, matcaps, emissions, decals, outlines, AudioLink, Light Volumes, LTCGI and VRSL work the same way in every one, with a few extra settings on the two world shaders. Only five blocks move around: Wet and Goo, with the Touch and Movement groups that drive them, Shading Options, the Fabric and Fur groups, Baked Lighting, and Triplanar Projection with Tiling Breakup.

  • VixenWear / Latex Ultra - The physically based latex finish, and the only one with the full liquid toolkit: it is the shader with Wet Effects, Goo Effects, Touch and Movement. No stylised shading, no fabric or fur.
  • VixForge / Toon - The cel and anime sibling. Adds Shading Options; drops Wet and Goo, which are liquid-latex tricks that do not fit a toon surface. Its Realistic style is a full physical pass, so you can stylise the body and keep realistic boots on the same avatar.
  • VixenWear / Clothing Pro - Garments. Adds Shading Options plus all three cloth blocks: Fabric, Surface Fur and Real Fur. Drops Wet and Goo.
  • VixForge / Fur Pro - Fur on its own, without the cloth sheen. Adds Shading Options, Surface Fur and Real Fur. Drops Wet, Goo and Fabric.
  • VixForge / World Surface - The world build, and the widest of the six. It keeps Wet Effects, Goo Effects, Touch and Movement, and adds Shading Options, Fabric, Surface Fur, Baked Lighting, Triplanar Projection and Tiling Breakup. No Real Fur.
  • VixForge / World Fur - The world build with the strands. Adds Shading Options, Fabric, Surface Fur, Real Fur, Baked Lighting, Triplanar Projection and Tiling Breakup. Drops Wet and Goo.

The Fabric, Surface Fur and Real Fur blocks are documented under SPECIAL EFFECTS, Shading Options and Baked Lighting under SHADING, and Triplanar Projection and Tiling Breakup under BASE, because that is where the inspector puts them.

Common Questions

FAQ

Which shader should I use?

Latex Ultra for latex, vinyl, rubber and anything else glossy and synthetic. VixForge Toon for cel and anime looks. Clothing Pro for fabric, from silk and satin through to wool and fleece. Fur Pro when the whole thing is fur. They share the same lighting and the same toolkit, so whatever you learn on one carries over.

Can I bring my materials over from Poiyomi, lilToon or VRChat Toon Standard?

Yes. The inspector translates an existing material across in one click, and it can convert a whole avatar at once. It takes a backup first and the whole thing is undoable, so you can try it and change your mind.

Do I need AudioLink, LTCGI, VRC Light Volumes or VRSL installed?

No. The shaders carry their own copies of what they need, so they work whether or not you have any of those packages. Each reaction stays off until you switch it on, and it then responds when a world actually provides the data.

Can I switch effects on and off from an expression menu?

Yes, that is what they were built for. Effects turn on and off live, so an animation or a menu toggle drives them in the world. One thing to remember: if you animate a control, tick Animated (when locked) on it before you lock the material, so it keeps responding afterwards.
